Close Together, Far Apart

A computer can learn to tell faces apart by turning each picture into a dot on a map. Here, every dot is one picture of a person. Same color means same person.

We want pictures of the same person close together, and pictures of different people farther apart.

A penalty is a score for how wrong the map is. Zero means the goal is met. While learning, the computer keeps nudging dots to make the penalty smaller. Below are two ways to score the map.

1 Comparing two pictures

Called contrastive loss. Look at one pair and check it against a fixed ruler.

Penalty 0

Distance apart: 0 steps

Drag either dot. Keyboard: tab to a dot and use the arrow keys. The arrows show which way the penalty pushes each dot.

Show the math

Let d be the distance between the two dots, and m = 3 the size of the circle (the margin).

same person:   penalty = d²

different people:   penalty = max(0, m − d)²

max(0, …) means “if this comes out negative, use 0 instead.” So once different people are at least m apart, they cost nothing.

Training adds this up over many pairs of pictures and moves the dots, a little at a time, to make the total smaller.

2 Comparing three pictures

Called triplet loss. No fixed ruler, just a comparison.

Your other picture should be closer to you than the stranger’s picture, with a little extra room.

The solid circle passes through your other picture. The dashed circle is one step bigger: that gap is the extra room. The stranger needs to be outside the dashed circle. Take a learning step moves all three dots a little, the way a computer does while learning.

Show the math

Call the three pictures a (you), p (another picture of you) and n (someone else). Let m = 1 be the extra room (the margin).

penalty = max(0, d(a, p) + m − d(a, n))

The penalty is zero when d(a, n) ≥ d(a, p) + m: the stranger is at least m farther away than your other picture.

A learning step moves each dot a small amount in the direction that lowers the penalty fastest (gradient descent). p moves toward a, n moves away from a, and a does a bit of both. Some versions square the distances. The idea is the same.

Why both work, and how they differ

1 Two pictures

  • Uses a fixed ruler. Same person: as close as possible. Different people: at least 3 steps apart.
  • The same-person penalty only reaches zero when the dots touch. It keeps pulling.
  • Each pair is judged on its own.

2 Three pictures

  • Uses a comparison. Your other picture just has to beat the stranger by the extra room.
  • Your two pictures don’t have to touch. Once the stranger is far enough, the penalty is zero and nothing moves.
  • It never asks “close enough?” It only asks “closer, and by enough?” Slide all three dots somewhere else together and nothing changes.

Both work for the same reason: every penalty pulls matching pictures together and pushes non-matching ones apart, until the rule is met. Do that for thousands of pictures, and each person’s pictures end up in their own neighborhood on the map.

Every number is computed on the page. A real system would use thousands of pictures and a learned map in many dimensions, not two.
