Closer Counts More

Four students told us how many hours they studied and what they scored. A new student wants to guess their own score. One way is to ask the four students, but give each one a different amount of say.

Students who studied about as long as you count the most.

The guess is a weighted average of the four scores. Each student gets a weight, a share of the say, and the shares always add up to 100%. Closer study time means a bigger share.

Show the math

You studied x hours. Student n studied xn hours and scored yn. First give each student a raw score that is 1 when they studied exactly as long as you, and shrinks the farther away they are (a bell curve, or Gaussian kernel):

rawn = e−(x − xn)² / (2h²)

Then divide by the total so the shares add up to 100%:

wn = rawn / (raw1 + raw2 + raw3 + raw4)

predicted score = w1y1 + w2y2 + w3y3 + w4y4

h is the bandwidth: how wide the bell is, in hours. Because the weights are shares of 100%, the prediction is always between the lowest score (50) and the highest (98).

What the slider does

Small h

  • A narrow bell. Only students who studied almost exactly as long as you count.
  • The prediction copies the closest student, and jumps when a different student becomes closest.

Large h

  • A wide bell. Students far away count almost as much as close ones.
  • Every student gets about 25%, so the prediction becomes the plain average, 77, no matter how long you studied.

In between is the useful part: close students matter most, and farther ones still help a little. Turn on Show every prediction and move the slider to see the whole curve go from jumpy to flat.

Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction, §16.3 (kernel density estimation and kernel regression, also called the Nadaraya–Watson model). Every number is computed on the page from the four students shown. A real model would use many more students, and h would be chosen by checking predictions on students it hasn’t seen.
