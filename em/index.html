Touch, Then Climb

We can write down the true score of a Gaussian mixture exactly, but finding where it peaks is hard. EM never climbs it directly. It builds an easier curve that sits underneath and touches it, climbs that, and repeats. This page shows every step with real numbers.

A simplified EM example that learns one centre. Fourteen fixed points, two clusters, both with variance σ² = 1 and weight π = ½. Cluster 2’s centre μ₂ stays where you put it. EM learns only μ₁, so the plots below show the whole problem, not a slice of it.

Where to start

Moving either centre restarts the run. Fixed throughout: σ₁ = σ₂ = 1, π₁ = π₂ = ½.

Things to try

  • A smooth climb. μ₂ sits on the left group; μ₁ starts in the gap and walks right in a few big steps, then tiny ones.
  • The gap grows before it shrinks. μ₁ has to cross μ₂’s group. Watch the gap bars: the second M step opens a much bigger gap than the first.
  • Stuck on a lower peak. μ₂ sits between the groups. Starting on the right, EM settles on the right group even though the left one scores higher.
  • Same setup, other side. Start on the left instead and EM finds the higher peak. The starting point decided it.

Why build a bound at all?

We know the true score exactly. For our 14 points it is

ℓ(μ₁) = Σn log [ ½N(yn | μ₁, 1) + ½N(yn | μ₂, 1) ]

The trouble is the log of a sum. Set its slope to zero and you get μ₁ = (weighted average of the points), where the weights are the responsibilities, which themselves depend on μ₁. The answer appears on both sides, so there is no formula to just solve.

EM breaks the loop. It freezes the weights as q, and then the bound is a simple parabola in μ₁ whose peak is the weighted average. That’s the M step:

μ₁ ← Σn qn1 yn / Σn qn1

Nothing is subtracted from the cluster equations to make this work. The bound differs from the true score by exactly the KL gap, and the identity on the right says so to the last decimal.

Reading the identity

ℓ(θ) − ℒ(θ, q) = Σn DKL( qn(zn) ‖ p(zn | yn, θ) )

θ
The model’s settings. Here only μ₁ ever changes.
ℓ(θ)
The true score: how probable the model says our data is, as a log. Higher is better. This is the observed-data log likelihood.
yn, zn
Point number n, and the hidden label saying which cluster made it. We see yn, never zn.
qn(zn)
Our belief about point n’s label: a probability for cluster 1 and one for cluster 2.
p(zn | yn, θ)
What the model, with its current settings, says that label probably is. These are the responsibilities.
ℒ(θ, q)
The lower bound (ELBO): a score we get by trusting q. Never above ℓ.
DKL( a ‖ b )
How far belief a is from b. Never negative, and zero exactly when they match.
Σn
Add that up over all 14 points.

What EM guarantees, and what it doesn’t

One M step, written as a chain:

ℓ(θnew) ≥ ℒ(θnew, q)

≥ ℒ(θold, q) = ℓ(θold)

The first ≥ is because KL is never negative. The second is because the M step picks the bound’s peak. The = is because the E step made q the posterior, so the gap was zero.

The true score never goes down.

  • Promised: ℓ does not decrease from one step to the next.
  • Not promised: that the KL gap shrinks step by step. It is zero after every E step and can open wider after a later M step than an earlier one.
  • Not promised: that μ₁ heads for the highest peak. It climbs whatever hill it starts on.
  • Not promised: the best answer. EM settles where the bound’s peak is where it already stands, which can be a lower peak.

Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction, §8.7.2–8.7.3 (the EM algorithm, its lower bound, EM for a GMM). Scores are natural logs (nats). Every curve and number is computed on the page: probabilities are combined with log-sum-exp, and the KL terms come straight from log posteriors, so nothing underflows even with a centre far from every point. A run counts as settled when an M step moves μ₁ by less than 0.0005.
