Same Votes, Different Teachers

A spam filter looks at an email and checks two words: “free” and “winner”. Naive Bayes and logistic regression both reach their answer the same way. Each clue casts a vote, the votes are added up, and the total becomes a probability. They differ in how they learn those votes.

Naive Bayes, worked by hand. 20% of emails are spam. “free” appears in 60% of spam and 10% of not-spam. “winner” appears in 50% of spam and 5% of not-spam. Naive Bayes assumes that, once you know whether an email is spam, the two words show up independently of each other.

Odds compare spam to not-spam. Odds of 0.25 mean 1 spam for every 4 not-spam. A word’s multiplier is how much more often it turns up in spam than in not-spam. A missing word is evidence too, so its multiplier uses the chance the word is absent, 1 − p.

P(spam) is the probability the email is spam. e ≈ 2.718 undoes the log, so 1 / (1 + e−z) turns votes back into odds, and odds into a probability. A total of z = 0 means a 50/50 call.

Naive Bayes learns the clues, then derives the votes

From labelled emails it counts the share of spam with “free”, the share of not-spam with “free”, and so on. These are feature probabilities within each class. The votes are then calculated from those counts, as in steps 1 and 2.

The first number holds the starting vote plus the votes for both words being absent. Each word’s weight is how much its vote changes when the word appears.

Logistic regression learns the votes directly

It uses the same form,

z = w0 + wfree·[free] + wwinner·[winner]

but never estimates how often a word appears in each class. It tunes the weights w until the predicted P(spam) fits the labels on the training emails as well as possible.

It makes no independence assumption. If two words tend to appear together, it can learn smaller weights so the same clue is not counted twice.

w0
The baseline vote, before looking at any word.
wfree, wwinner
The vote each word adds when it appears.
[free]
1 if the email contains “free”, 0 if not.
